# Name the destination, not the gesture

The evidence and the guidance point the same way. A link's visible words should name where it goes or what it does, and the information-carrying words should come first. Bare "Click here" is banned by every source that mentions it. Bare "More" and "Read more" fail every source's general test, which is whether the link makes sense out of context. **"Learn more" is the one genuinely contested case.** Apple's HIG holds up "Learn more about UX Writing" as its good example. Shopify Polaris allows a bare trailing "Learn more" after a sentence that names the topic. Microsoft's Windows UI guidance calls both forms "not our style" but allows one when context is clear. Atlassian, Mailchimp, Intuit and Fluent 2 discourage it. The consensus is much stronger than the measured evidence behind it. The direct measurement amounts to one 2009 Nielsen comprehension test (85% against 15%), an academic model of navigation failure from 2002, and eye-tracking showing that people read about the first two words of a link. **No controlled study compares "Learn more" with a descriptive label on click-through or task success**, and none of the evidence comes from desktop apps. Two constraints sharpen this for a Mac app that opens web documentation in about 20 languages. First, a native control has no equivalent of HTML's "programmatically determined context", so the label has to stand alone. Second, short labels expand by 200–300% in translation, so the label must be a complete string with wrapping room, and any arrow glyph must sit outside it.

## The measured core is one comprehension test and a theory

The claim with the strongest evidence is about position, not vocabulary. In Jakob Nielsen's 2009 study, **80 participants saw only the first 11 characters of 20 real links and headlines**. A specific, front-loaded label ("Gift Cards & E-Gift Certificates") was understood by 85%. A generic opener ("Introducing…") was understood by 15%. Nielsen named the three "nasties" as bland generic words, made-up terms, and deferring the information-carrying text to the end ([NN/g, Nielsen 2009](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/first-2-words-a-signal-for-scanning/)). NN/g's later guidance builds on this. "People mostly look at the first 2 words of a link." Eye-tracking shows an F-shaped scan followed by heavy fixation on links ([NN/g, McCloskey 2014](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/writing-links/); [NN/g, F-shaped pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content/)). Note what this measures: **comprehension from a fragment, not clicks**.

Information foraging theory supplies the mechanism. Pirolli and Card model users as maximising the rate at which they gain valuable information, and they judge that value from proximal cues, link labels foremost among them. Vague cues lower the perceived value, and users either pick the wrong path or abandon it ([PhilPapers, Pirolli & Card 1999](https://philpapers.org/rec/PIRIF); [NN/g, Budiu 2020](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/information-foraging/)). The Cognitive Walkthrough for the Web operationalised this with Latent Semantic Analysis. It found **unfamiliar and confusable labels to be predictable classes of navigation failure** ([ACM DL, Blackmon et al. 2002](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/503376.503459)). A follow-up reported that repairing those labels improved success, though the effect sizes were seen only through a search summary ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/221514993_Repairing_usability_problems_identified_by_the_Cognitive_Walkthrough_for_the_Web)). Under this theory, "Learn more" carries almost no scent of its own. It borrows all of it from nearby text. That predicts a small penalty under a strong heading and a real one wherever the link is met alone.

A 2022 PLOS ONE study adds a caution in the other direction. Readers rated linked sentences as **more important regardless of content**, and skim-reading cut comprehension from 91% to 86% ([Jayes et al. 2022](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0263669)). Link styling claims attention whatever the words say, so a generic link spends that attention on nothing.

Several widely repeated figures do not survive checking. Jared Spool's clickstream numbers (58% failure overall, more than 80% after the back button, 98% after two back clicks) are relayed only through a liveblog, with no published method ([Adactio](https://adactio.com/journal/4539)). The popular claim that **7–12-word links succeed far more often** traces to a UIE report from around 2004 that could not be read, so treat it as **unverified** ([UX Tigers](https://www.uxtigers.com/post/information-scent)). A claim that vague links are ignored "up to 50%" more has no traceable study and should not be cited. Conversion-blog A/B results such as "+90% click-through" vary pronoun, value and urgency all at once, and they do not isolate generic against descriptive ([OptinMonster](https://optinmonster.com/8-ab-tests-to-run-on-your-popups-to-get-more-email-subscribers/)). The honest summary is that **a strong theory plus one 2009 test carries a near-universal consensus**. A click-through literature does not.

## Accessibility sets a floor at Level A and a ceiling at AAA

WCAG 2.2 **SC 2.4.4 Link Purpose (In Context) is Level A**, not AA as it is often misquoted, so every AA procurement standard includes it. A link passes if its purpose can be determined "from the link text alone or from the link text together with its programmatically determined link context." That context means the same sentence, paragraph, list item or table cell, or an `aria-label`/`aria-labelledby` ([WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/); [Understanding 2.4.4](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-in-context.html)). A bare "Learn more" inside its explaining paragraph is therefore conformant. One sitting alone below a heading relies on H80, which is only an *advisory* technique, and **failure F63 explicitly fails a "Read More…" in a paragraph after the one it refers to** ([F63](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/failures/F63)). **SC 2.4.9 (AAA)** requires the link text alone to suffice. F84 fails "click here" and "more" when there is no mechanism to change them, because "many blind people who use screen readers call up a dialog box that has a list of links" ([F84](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/failures/F84); [Understanding 2.4.9](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/link-purpose-link-only.html)).

That rationale needs a correction in proportion. WebAIM's 2024 survey of 1,539 screen-reader users found that only **4.8% first look for content by links, against 71.6% who use headings** ([WebAIM SRUS #10](https://webaim.org/projects/screenreadersurvey10/)). This measures the *first* strategy, not how often people use a links list or the VoiceOver rotor ([Apple Support](https://support.apple.com/guide/voiceover/use-the-voiceover-rotor-mchlp2719/mac)). Users who start with headings still tab from link to link, and at that point five "Learn more" controls sound identical.

The trap people most often fall into is the repair, not the original problem. **SC 2.5.3 Label in Name (Level A)** requires the accessible name to contain the visible text, and best practice puts that text at the start. The reason is that speech-input users say what they see ([Understanding 2.5.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/label-in-name.html)). So `aria-label="AutoCode documentation"` on a visible "Learn more" is a **hard Level A failure (F96)**. The W3C's own ARIA8 example avoids this by beginning the label with the visible words ("Read more about Seminole tax hike") ([ARIA8](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/aria/ARIA8)). WebAIM, Deque, MDN and GOV.UK all prefer descriptive *visible* text. They fall back to hidden text that completes the visible phrase only when space forces a short label ([WebAIM](https://webaim.org/techniques/hypertext/link_text); [Deque](https://www.deque.com/blog/text-links-practices-screen-readers/); [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/a); [GOV.UK Design System](https://design-system.service.gov.uk/styles/links/)). An aria-label fixes the screen reader and does nothing for the sighted scanner or the cognitively loaded reader, who are the people the NN/g evidence is about.

Native macOS changes the calculation. WCAG does not formally govern a Mac app, but audits apply it by analogy. A SwiftUI `Link` takes its accessible name from its label view and opens the URL in the default browser ([Apple: SwiftUI Link](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/swiftui/link)). Nothing ties it to a sibling heading the way HTML containment does, so in practice **the AAA bar, a label that stands alone, is the working bar in a native window**. Apple's HIG says only to "label interface elements appropriately" for Voice Control and VoiceOver ([Apple HIG: Accessibility](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/accessibility)). No Apple text addresses generic link labels. Arrow glyphs are an open question. No authoritative source documents how VoiceOver, NVDA or JAWS pronounce → or ↗. Practitioner reports say decorative arrows get announced unless hidden ([Yale Usability](https://usability.yale.edu/web-accessibility/articles/links), search summary only), while 2.5.3 notes that "most punctuation" is ignored when a label is spoken. A label like "Setup →" could therefore be read aloud as "Setup right arrow". **Test this on hardware rather than assuming either way.**

## Style guides agree on "click here" and split three ways on "Learn more"

| Guide | "Click here" / "More" | "Learn more" | Preferred form |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apple HIG | Avoid "Click here" | **Good example: "Learn more about UX Writing"** | Verb for buttons and links |
| Shopify Polaris | Never "click here" / "here" | **Bare trailing "Learn more" after a topic sentence; max one per screen; "Learn more about X" a Don't when it repeats context** | Embedded link inside the sentence |
| Microsoft Windows UI text | "More information about…" not our style | Bare and "about X" both "not our style", but "OK… if the context is clear" | Full sentence or question as the link |
| Microsoft Fluent 2 | No "Click here" or URLs | Avoid without saying what it's about | Predictable purpose |
| IBM Carbon | Make "read more" unique | Tolerated with `aria-labelledby`/`describedby` | Distinctive; no shared text across destinations |
| Google dev docs, GOV.UK | Avoid "click here", "more" | Fails their out-of-context test | Page title or topic; "For more information, see [Title]" |
| Mailchimp, Atlassian, Intuit | Avoid | **Avoid / "filler"** | Describe action or destination |

Sources: [Apple HIG Writing](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/writing); [Polaris grammar-and-mechanics](https://github.com/Shopify/polaris/blob/main/polaris.shopify.com/content/content/grammar-and-mechanics.mdx); [Windows Admin Center UI text guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/manage/windows-admin-center/extend/guides/ui-text-style-guide); [Fluent 2 Link](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/components/web/react/core/link/usage); [Carbon Link accessibility](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/link/accessibility/); [Google link text](https://developers.google.com/style/link-text); [GOV.UK Add links](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/add-links/); [Mailchimp](https://styleguide.mailchimp.com/writing-for-accessibility/); [Atlassian Forge](https://developer.atlassian.com/platform/forge/action-components-guidelines/); [Intuit word list](https://contentdesign.intuit.com/word-list/).

The disagreement over "Learn more" has a pattern. The **UI-native systems** (Apple HIG, Polaris, Microsoft's Windows guidance) tolerate some form of "Learn more" because their links live on space-constrained surfaces next to a topic sentence. The **documentation and government guides** (Google, GOV.UK) do not, because their links have to survive out of context. Two specific conflicts matter. Apple's "Learn more about X" is exactly what Polaris marks a Don't when X has already been named. And Microsoft's Windows guidance makes a whole question the link ("How does Windows Firewall help protect my computer?"), while Google says not to make a sentence the link. **Length guidance also varies**: the Microsoft Writing Style Guide asks for four words or fewer ([Microsoft Style Guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/style-guide/search-writing)), while Intuit allows 8 characters to about 55 ([Intuit Actions](https://contentdesign.intuit.com/product-and-ui/actions/)). Underneath these disputes, the guides agree on more than they appear to. Action links lead with a verb ("Send a tax return"). Information links use the destination's topic or title. The same destination gets the same text, and different destinations get different text ([GOV.UK](https://guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk/writing-to-gov-uk-standards/writing-guidelines/add-links/); [Polaris link.mdx](https://github.com/Shopify/polaris/blob/main/polaris.shopify.com/content/components/navigation/link.mdx)). Some practitioner positions in the brief could not be verified. Krug's rule that a page's name should match the words clicked to reach it is seen only in secondary summaries ([Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/work/quotes/561281-don-t-make-me-think-a-common-sense-approach-to-web-usability)). No passage on link text could be found in Yifrah, Podmajersky or Redish, beyond the fact that Redish's book has a "Writing Meaningful Links" chapter ([Elsevier](https://shop.elsevier.com/books/letting-go-of-the-words/redish/978-0-12-385930-3)).

## Leaving the app warrants a warning in words, not an ellipsis

No source addresses an in-app link that opens a web browser, which leaves the nearest rules to be adapted. Apple treats help as rare and contextual: one help button per window, opening "the help topic that's related to the current context", with no text introducing it ([HIG Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons)). Microsoft's answer to "when should we provide a Help link?" is "**Almost never**". Only offer one when there is an obvious, important question and no room in the UI to answer it, and route it through a redirect service rather than a raw URL, so the link survives docs reorganisations ([Windows Admin Center guide](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows-server/manage/windows-admin-center/extend/guides/ui-text-style-guide)). Polaris says to "avoid links if possible" because they make software "feel… like an old-fashioned webpage" ([Polaris](https://github.com/Shopify/polaris/blob/main/polaris.shopify.com/content/content/grammar-and-mechanics.mdx)).

On signalling departure, GOV.UK puts the destination in the words ("Find a counsellor on Counselling Directory"). NN/g and Fluent 2 ask for both an icon and descriptive text before the click when a link opens a new window ([NN/g 2020](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/new-browser-windows-and-tabs/); [Fluent 2](https://fluent2.microsoft.design/components/web/react/core/link/usage)). Carbon uses a "launch" icon for external links and a right arrow for internal ones ([Carbon Link usage](https://carbondesignsystem.com/components/link/usage/)). That convention supports ↗ over → for "leaves the app". The claim that most users don't understand external-link icons is **unverified**, a search summary that blends a blog with NN/g ([hey.georgie.nu](https://hey.georgie.nu/hyperlinks/)), so the icon should be a secondary cue at most. On macOS specifically, **do not use a trailing ellipsis**. Apple defines it as meaning the user will "provide additional input" in another window, and a docs page asks for none ([HIG Buttons](https://developer.apple.com/design/human-interface-guidelines/buttons)).

## Translation turns short labels into the riskiest strings in the UI

Short English strings expand the most. IBM's figures, quoted by the W3C, give **200–300% for strings up to 10 characters and 180–200% for 11–20 characters**. The W3C advises against abbreviating to fit and against small fixed-width containers ([W3C i18n](https://www.w3.org/International/articles/article-text-size)). A 14-character label can plausibly come back at 25–28 characters. Truncating it with "…" cuts off exactly the destination words a descriptive label exists to carry. Microsoft calls concatenation "one of the most challenging issues for translators to resolve". Word order, separators, agreement and declension all break templates assembled from fragments, so each label must be one complete string ([Microsoft Globalization](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/globalization/internationalization/concatenation)). Grammatical form also shifts between languages. German UI convention prefers the infinitive, "Kanäle verwalten" rather than "Verwalte Kanäle" ([Zulip German guide](https://zulip.readthedocs.io/en/stable/translating/german.html)). By inference, noun-phrase labels naming a destination should move between languages with less change of register than English imperatives. No verified source covers Japanese, Korean or Chinese link conventions, so that inference needs checking against Apple's per-locale loctables. Directional glyphs need mirroring for right-to-left scripts, which is one more reason to keep them out of the string ([Mozilla l10n](https://mozilla-l10n.github.io/documentation/localization/dev_best_practices.html), search summary only).

## Principles that follow

| Principle | Basis | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Put the destination's name in the first two words; spend no words on "Learn more about", "Click here to", "Introducing" | Nielsen 2009; NN/g 2014; foraging theory | Measured (comprehension) + theory |
| For a docs link, use the docs page's own title or topic as a noun phrase; for a task, lead with a verb | GOV.UK, Google, Microsoft, Polaris | Consensus, asserted |
| In a native window, write the label to stand alone; don't rely on a sibling heading | 2.4.9 rationale; no programmatic context in SwiftUI | Inference from standards |
| If a short visible label must stay, make the accessible name *begin with* the visible words | WCAG 2.5.3, F96, ARIA8 | Normative (Level A) |
| Same destination, same text; different destinations, different text; at most one generic link per view | WCAG 2.4.9 intent; Polaris; Carbon | Consensus |
| Signal leaving the app in words; treat ↗ as a secondary cue; never an ellipsis | GOV.UK; NN/g; Fluent; Apple ellipsis definition | Guidance; icon comprehension unverified |
| One complete string per label, with the glyph rendered outside it and room to wrap at ~2× English width | W3C/IBM expansion; Microsoft concatenation | Measured (expansion) + guidance |
| Don't promise a localised destination the docs can't deliver; mark English-only pages | Title-matching rule | Inference |

## Conclusion

The debate over "Learn more" is less about the phrase than about where the context lives. Every guide that tolerates it does so because a topic sentence sits immediately before it, programmatically or visually. Every guide that rejects it assumes the link will be met alone. A Mac app's Welcome card is the second case wearing the clothes of the first. The heading looks adjacent, but VoiceOver, Voice Control and the scanning eye each meet the control on its own, and the context will not survive translation into a layout whose labels have doubled in length. The decision therefore turns out to be simple. The label should name the docs page, and it should do so in the first words a translator can reorder freely.

Two things remain genuinely unknown, and both are cheap to settle locally rather than by more reading. The first is how VoiceOver and Voice Control treat a trailing → or ↗ in a SwiftUI label. The second is whether destination-named labels outperform "Learn more" for this app's users at all, since no published study has measured click-through or task success for the distinction. The field's confidence rests on theory and a 2009 fragment test. A five-participant tree test, or a first-click test on the Welcome screen, would produce more decision-relevant evidence than the whole literature currently offers.
