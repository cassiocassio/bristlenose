# Task mining's OCR templates chart the Screens lens

No shipped product or published paper was found that infers screens from third-party usability recordings (Zoom, Teams, Meet screen shares) and then files participants' quotes under those screens. A Bristlenose Screens lens would therefore be new in what it combines, though none of its parts is new. Every tool that organises findings by screen today owns the stimulus. Maze and its peers import a Figma prototype. UserTesting's Interactive Path Flows record navigation in Chrome. Contentsquare, FullStory and Hotjar inject a JS tag. Tools that accept arbitrary recordings, such as Dovetail, Condens and dscout, analyse only the transcript. The technical pieces are published in other fields. Mobile bug-video research (GIFdroid, ViBR, Feiz et al.) handles keyframes and screen identity. Robotic process mining handles OCR template clustering, and UiPath's patent is close to a blueprint for it. Web-crawler research (Yandrapally et al., Rico) handles the near-duplicate question. Usability metrics and event-sequence visualisation handle small-sample path display (expected path, lostness, Treejack's pietree, prefix trees). Three findings carry most of the weight. Deciding "same screen" is a product decision about granularity more than a threshold to tune, and thresholds measurably fail to transfer between apps. OCR text alone separates screens only moderately well (F1 ≈ 0.65), so it needs layout and a visual signal beside it. The analytics industry's default view, a Sankey or directly-follows graph with frequency pruning, is the wrong form for 5–12 participants, because it invents loops and hides individuals.

## Every screen-organised tool owns the stimulus; nobody reads the pixels

The survey splits cleanly in two. Tools that show findings per screen get screen identity for free, by instrumenting what is shown. Maze defines a *path* as "the flow of screens a participant actually takes", and it scores each screen (SCUS) against an expected path from an imported prototype ([Maze glossary](https://help.maze.co/articles/7088609152-maze-glossary)). Loop11 users give each prototype page its own URL so the tool can track it ([Axure forum](https://forum.axure.com/t/usertesting-with-loop11/55743)). The closest commercial analogue to a Screens lens is **UserTesting's Interactive Path Flows**, launched in October 2020. It aggregates the screens visited across participants into a flow with thumbnails, flags uncommon paths, and links every step to its video segment ([UserTesting blog](https://usertesting.com/blog/interactive-path-flows)). It is built from navigation data, **requires Chrome on desktop, covers web only**, and finds screens by URL and page title ([UserTesting help](https://help.usertesting.com/hc/articles/11880401549853)). Session-replay tools go one step further and roll URLs up into named templates, which Contentsquare calls "page groups" inside a "mapping" ([Contentsquare API docs](https://docs.contentsquare.com/en/api/metrics/)). All of it rests on a URL and DOM stream that a screen-share video does not carry.

The other half ingests any recording but never looks at the screen. Dovetail's AI covers chat, highlights, clustering and summaries. Its only visual ML is face blurring ([Dovetail docs](https://docs.dovetail.com/help/dovetail-ai)). Great Question and Condens treat a highlight as a span of transcript with a matching clip ([Great Question](https://greatquestion.co/support/repository/how-to-use-and-share-higlights); [Condens](https://condens.io/help/using-condens/structuring-and-analyzing/structuring-data-with-tags)). dscout segments its usability video by *study question*, not by screen ([dscout help](https://help.dscout.com/hc/en-us/articles/36373180043412)). Even the HCI think-aloud analytics line (VisTA, CoUX, uxSense) mines audio, transcript and body pose. uxSense, on inspection of the full text, does no OCR and identifies no screen ([arXiv 2310.07300](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2310.07300)).

Reading screen-share pixels is now ordinary; using them to index findings is not. **Zoom AI Companion ships a host setting, "Use screen share content with OCR"**, which feeds shared-screen text into meeting summaries ([Brown University IT](https://ithelp.brown.edu/kb/articles/zoom-ai-companion-overview)). Teams recap puts screen-share markers on the timeline ([Microsoft Support](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/teams/meetings/recap-in-microsoft-teams)). AI-moderated research tools such as Outset say their moderator "can actually see what the participant sees", but they describe only real-time probing and topline synthesis, with no screen index ([Outset](https://outset.ai/almanac/ai-usability-testing-in-2026-the-workflow-that-works)). An industry round-up found no tool advertising automatic detection of which app screen is showing in a session video ([CleverX 2026](https://cleverx.com/blog/ai-usability-testing-tools-the-best-options-in-2026/)). These negative findings rest on vendor documentation and searches, not hands-on trials. Outset and Userology are the most likely to ship a per-screen view next, and should be re-checked.

## "Same screen" is a granularity decision, and thresholds do not travel

The most important prior art for the identity problem comes from web-crawler testing, where it has been measured carefully. Yandrapally, Stocco and Mesbah labelled state pairs from 1,031 websites as **clone, near-duplicate, or distinct**. In a random 1,000 pairs they found 441 clones, 275 near-duplicates and 284 distinct. **219 of the 275 near-duplicates were "same template, different data"** ([ICSE 2020](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/astocco/pubs/2020-Yandrapally-ICSE2020.pdf)). They tested ten similarity techniques on DOM, text hashes and pixels. The best three-class macro-F1 was only **0.60 (PDiff)**, and SimHash was worst at 0.33. Every technique did about **15% worse on apps its threshold had not been tuned on**, and app-specific thresholds improved model quality by 34% over universal ones. The authors concluded that "universal thresholds may not necessarily be feasible". Errors also compound: a crawler that began at F1 0.95 ended with a model at 0.45 as near-duplicates accumulated ([same](https://people.ece.ubc.ca/astocco/pubs/2020-Yandrapally-ICSE2020.pdf)). A learned per-app classifier, WebEmbed, reaches F1 0.96, but only with labelled pairs from that app ([arXiv 2306.07400](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.07400v2)). That labelling is unavailable when the product under test belongs to someone else.

The Android GUI-testing literature reaches the same conclusion from the other direction. Baek and Bae set out a five-rung ladder of equivalence: package, activity, layout widgets, interactive widgets, then text content. Moving from activity level to the finest level that stays feasible took Google Translate **from 5 to 55 nodes** and raised class coverage from 45% to 65%. At the text-content rung, one continuously changing TextView exploded the state count ([Baek & Bae, ASE 2016](https://aminer.org/pub/57d063feac4436735429811a)). DroidBot keeps two hashes in parallel: a content-sensitive one, and a content-free `structure_str` that drops text so same-template screens collapse ([DroidBot source](https://github.com/honeynet/droidbot/blob/master/droidbot/device_state.py)). APE does not fix the granularity at all. It refines or coarsens the abstraction during exploration, using whether merged states behave consistently ([APE, ICSE 2019](https://swag.uwaterloo.ca/publications/practical-gui-testing-of-android-applications-via-model-abstraction-and-refinement.html)). **Rico states the product decision outright**: database-backed apps "can have thousands of views that represent the same semantic concept and differ only in their content". So Rico treats screenshots differing by under 0.2% of pixels as the same, and otherwise as different only if their sets of resource IDs differ by more than one element. That rule had a 6% false-positive and 3% false-negative rate on 1,044 labelled pairs ([Rico, UIST 2017](https://www.ranjithakumar.net/resources/rico.pdf)).

For Bristlenose this reframes the problem. "Product page: blue kettle" and "Product page: red toaster" are one screen or two depending on what the researcher is studying, not on a similarity score. The defensible defaults follow from the evidence. Cluster within a study rather than against a global threshold. Default to template level, as Rico and DroidBot's content-free hash do. Let the researcher split and merge, as APE's adaptive refinement suggests and as UiPath's editable, unsupervised step labels already do in a shipping product ([UiPath docs](https://docs.uipath.com/task-mining/automation-suite/2023.10/user-guide/faq)). APE's signal also has a qualitative counterpart. A merged screen whose quotes or onward transitions disagree sharply is a candidate for over-merging. Two screens that occupy the same position in every participant's flow are candidates for under-merging.

## Layer cheap change detection, OCR templates and a visual tiebreaker

The measured comparisons of identity signals agree with each other. On mobile UI keyframes, GIFdroid found whole-frame perceptual hashes weak: **Precision@1 was 38.6% for aHash, 46.6% for pHash and 50.6% for dHash**, against 75.7% for SSIM or ORB alone and **85.4% for an equal-weighted SSIM+ORB blend** ([GIFdroid, ICSE 2022](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.04128)). The only direct measurement of OCR-based screen grouping found is ScreenSearch (2026). It reports a same-state pair **F1 of 0.646 from OCR**, against 0.740 from accessibility features, and judges OCR "a viable input" ([arXiv 2605.16024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.16024)). Screen2Vec's ablation points the same way: text-only embeddings predict the next screen far worse than text plus layout (top-1 0.012 against 0.061) ([Screen2Vec, CHI 2021](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/2101.11103)). Global text fingerprints have a specific weakness here. Different screens of one site share most of their boilerplate, so Henzinger found shingling and simhash both fail within a site even though both are precise across sites ([Manku et al., WWW 2007](https://research.google.com/pubs/archive/33026.pdf)). The best pixels-only classifier published, Feiz et al. at Apple, reaches **F1 0.83 for "same screen" within an iPhone app**. It also detects keyboard, dialog and scroll transitions at F1 0.71 ([Apple ML Research](https://machinelearning.apple.com/research/screen-relationships)). It is mobile-only, and its model is not known to be released.

The recipe that fits Bristlenose's inputs comes from **robotic process mining**. Vendors there fall back to screenshot OCR wherever instrumentation is impossible, such as Citrix and RDP. UiPath's FAQ says discovery "usually works" in that setting because it relies on OCR ([UiPath FAQ](https://docs.uipath.com/task-mining/automation-suite/2023.10/user-guide/faq)). That is the same epistemic position as a third-party call recording. UiPath's patent US11507772B2 reduces each screenshot to (word, normalised box) pairs and treats boxes as the same when **IoU exceeds 0.9**. A word-and-box item enters a cluster's **template** if it appears in more than **70%** of the cluster's members, and positions holding variable data become placeholders. Screens are clustered as binary template-membership vectors. Consecutive same-screen events are dropped. Rare words carried from one screen to the next raise the probability that the two are linked ([Google Patents](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11507772B2/en)). This solves two problems that defeat hashes. A changed price or name falls into a placeholder rather than creating a new screen. And shared rare data (a booking reference, a product name) becomes evidence of a real transition. The Seville ScreenRPA group likewise clusters events "by textual and visual screen features" ([CEUR Vol-4032](https://ceur-ws.org/Vol-4032/paper-03.pdf)), but publishes no accuracy numbers, and nor does any vendor.

Lecture-video work supplies the noise handling that call recordings need. TalkMiner, working on lossy webcasts with picture-in-picture speaker insets, ignores changes whose bounding box is smaller than a third of the frame and away from the centre. It takes a keyframe only after **3 s of stability**, and merges slide builds. Together these raised keyframe **precision from 47.5% to 76.9%**, while recall moved only from 91.7% to 94.0%. **Naive differencing produced about twice as many keyframes as the ground truth** ([TalkMiner, ACM MM 2010](https://people.csail.mit.edu/hpirsiav/papers/talkminer_acm_mm10.pdf)). That rule maps onto Teams and Zoom participant tiles, cursor motion and notification toasts, and it can be applied in text space by ignoring OCR changes confined to a small region at the frame's edge. Two further transfers fit Bristlenose. Text identity should down-weight tokens present on most frames of a session: global nav, footer, browser chrome and the call app's own UI. This is the text equivalent of Crawljax's comparators that strip ads and timestamps before comparison ([Crawljax source](https://github.com/crawljax/crawljax/tree/master/core/src/main/java/com/crawljax/oraclecomparator/comparators)), and the same move as Fluxicon's removal of "spider" activities that can occur anywhere ([Fluxicon](https://fluxicon.com/book/read/simplification/)). And an OCR'd address bar or window title, when visible, is a near-exact screen key. Task-mining vendors key on URL and window title through instrumentation ([UiPath](https://docs.uipath.com/task-mining/automation-suite/2022.4/user-guide/information-about-the-collected-data); [Celonis](https://docs.celonis.com/en/web-page-data-extractions.html)), but no published work measures it from video. It should override other signals when present and never be the only key, because tab shares, full-screen browsers, single-page apps and PII in query strings all defeat or contaminate it.

The 4-second sampling cadence changes which parts of the bug-video literature apply. GIFdroid's "steady for five frames" rule at 7–30 fps is a window of about 0.2–0.7 s, so at 4 s a single unchanged sample already counts as steady. The real risk runs the other way. A screen held for less than about 4 s is never sampled, and two transitions inside one interval merge into one. Waken's screencast study gives the matching warning about actions: click recall was **29% overall but 66% on 24 fps video** ([Waken, UIST 2012](https://www.research.autodesk.com/app/uploads/2023/03/waken-reverse-engineering-usage.pdf_rec2V6wlIVQVxnwlr.pdf)). At 4 s, Bristlenose should infer *screen visits*, not *clicks*. Generic shot-boundary detectors are no shortcut: PySceneDetect scored F1 0.475 on UI keyframes, against GIFdroid's 0.880 ([GIFdroid](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2112.04128)). One option is to reuse the 4 fps scene strips Bristlenose already computes in serve mode to locate change boundaries densely, while keeping the 4 s OCR for identity. The literature suggests this, but nobody has tested it on call recordings. Finally, a frontier vision-language model can play the Feiz or ViBR role at the cluster level, naming each cluster and adjudicating borderline merges, rather than comparing every sample pair. ViBR shows the approach working on screen-capture video with no pre-built graph, reproducing 72% of bug recordings ([arXiv 2604.19905](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.19905)).

## Analytics flow charts mislead at n of ten; trees, swimlanes and expected paths do not

The product-analytics vendors have converged on one model, and much of it is built for volume. Mixpanel shows the top 3 events per step and an "Other" bar ([Mixpanel](https://docs.mixpanel.com/docs/reports/flows)). GA4 shows the top 5 nodes per step, up to 20, then "Others", and can anchor a start *or* an end point but not both ([GA4 help](https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9317498)). At five to twelve participants, an "Other" bucket hides whole people. One convention transfers exactly: **collapse consecutive repeats but keep revisits**. Mixpanel ("Collapse Repeated"), GA4 ("View unique nodes only"), Adobe (repeat instances off by default) and Pendo all merge A,A,A into A and keep A,B,A ([Adobe](https://experienceleague.adobe.com/en/docs/analytics/analyze/analysis-workspace/visualizations/flow/create-flow); [Pendo](https://adoptsupport.pendo.io/hc/en-us/articles/4408364979483-Paths-Overview)). Under this rule a screen held for four minutes and sampled sixty times is one visit. Every one of these vendors also pairs its Sankey with a ranked list of whole paths, which shows that the flow chart alone cannot answer "what did each person actually do".

Process mining warns against the obvious alternative, a screen graph. Van der Aalst shows that directly-follows graphs, the "de facto standard" in commercial tools, produce **spurious loops whenever ordering is flexible**, even when every activity happens at most once. Frequency-pruned edges create "invisible gaps", where node counts stop equalling the sum of their in- and out-edges. In his worked example, filtering the graph rather than the log turns "a is always followed by c in 6 days" into "a→c half the time, in 10 days", and Disco and Celonis both produced the misleading version ([van der Aalst 2019](https://www.vdaalst.com/publications/p1101.pdf)). In a usability session, back-navigation and free exploration are exactly the "flexible ordering" that turns a graph into spaghetti, and the loops then read as confusion. If a graph is offered at all, its edges should be built from complete per-participant sequences, never pruned, and labelled with participant counts so the sums hold.

The forms that suit small samples are the ones usability practice already uses, plus prefix trees from visualisation research. Treejack's **pietree** sizes each node by the number of participants and colours on-path against off-path. Its *directness* counts participants who reached the answer without backtracking ([Lupa.cz](https://www.lupa.cz/clanky/pouzitelnost-s-treejackem-ztraci-se-vam-na-webu-uzivatele-otestujte-navigaci/)). Maze groups paths into direct success, indirect success and unfinished against a declared expected path ([Maze 101](https://maze.co/guides/maze-101-guide/reporting/)). **Smith's lostness** needs only one participant's screen sequence: total screens, unique screens, and the minimum required. It flags above 0.5 as lost, below 0.4 as not lost. Smith's own follow-up found a participant above 0.5 who was *exploring*, not lost ([MeasuringU](https://measuringu.com/?p=537)). CoreFlow and LifeFlow aggregate sequences into a branching tree, so divergence appears as a fork and a revisit as a repeated node further along the branch, never as an ambiguous cycle ([CoreFlow](https://www.zcliu.cs.umd.edu/coreflow); [LifeFlow](https://www.cs.umd.edu/projects/hcil/lifeflow)). At n≈10 such a tree has at most ten leaves. The one head-to-head study found people made **fewer errors with MatrixWave's transition matrices than with a Sankey, and preferred it** ([MatrixWave, CHI 2015](https://mail.zcliu.cs.umd.edu/matrixwave/matrixwave-chi.pdf)). Outflow's outcome colouring, green for success and red for failure, maps onto task outcomes ([Outflow, InfoVis 2012](https://vaclab.unc.edu/publication/tvcg_2012_gotz/tvcg_2012_gotz.pdf)).

None of these techniques has been evaluated on fewer than twenty sequences, and that sets a limit on what Bristlenose should claim. With ten participants, every path is likely a unique variant: even at scale, 30% of process variants occur only once ([van der Aalst 2019](https://www.vdaalst.com/publications/p1101.pdf)). So "the typical order" cannot be mined in any statistical sense. The honest presentation sets the researcher's expected order, taken from the discussion guide's tasks, against each participant's actual order. That means a per-participant swimlane, with participants as rows and screens as labelled spans in time, plus a prefix tree or pietree that counts participants ("7 of 9") rather than events. Fluxicon's milestone abstraction gives the nesting: tasks as milestones, screens within them ([Fluxicon](https://fluxicon.com/book/read/simplification/)). The swimlane recommendation is an inference from the evidence above, not a tested result.

## Quotes on screens is the unclaimed step, and it resolves path ambiguity

Every tool reviewed stops short of attaching coded quotes to screens. UserTesting links a path-flow step to a **video segment** ([UserTesting blog](https://usertesting.com/blog/interactive-path-flows)). Contentsquare lets you right-click a page group into Session Replay ([Contentsquare support](https://support.contentsquare.com/hc/en-us/articles/37271868172817)). Maze's per-screen view is quantitative (time, misclicks, SCUS). The research principle that most supports going further is Liu et al.'s "patterns and sequences" design: never show an aggregate without coordinated access to the raw sequences beneath it ([Adobe Research](https://research.adobe.com/publication/patterns-and-sequences-interactive-exploration-of-clickstreams-to-understand-common-visitor-paths)). In Bristlenose the raw evidence is the quote, the tag and the sentiment, not just the clip.

This is where small-n qualitative data stops being a weakness. The two warnings above converge: van der Aalst's flexible ordering is not looping, and Smith's exploring is not being lost. A revisit or a high lostness score is ambiguous from the path alone, and what the participant said on that screen decides it. Lifting quotes into the flow view is therefore not decoration. It is the evidence that makes path metrics interpretable at n≈10, and the session-replay and analytics tools cannot supply it. Spider screens show the same logic from the other side. The call app, the desktop, a global nav overlay and the home screen should be excluded from the order calculation, but their quotes must stay reachable, because "I can't find the menu" is often said on exactly those screens.

The alignment rule itself is the least-evidenced part. A natural default assigns a quote to the screen showing at its start timecode, but no study was found that measures how far think-aloud speech leads or lags the screen in remote sessions. The nearest evidence is Murali et al. Participants' gaze landed on the webpage content they were verbalising significantly more often than their mouse did, and the mouse was "mostly used passively to scroll" ([arXiv 2307.05171](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.05171)). That supports anchoring to the visible screen rather than to cursor position, which call video does not reliably provide anyway. It says nothing about timing. A quote that spans a transition should show both screens rather than being forced into one, and the boundary rule should be something the researcher can see and override.

## Applicability: what transfers to recordings, and what needs instrumentation

| Prior art | Needs instrumentation? | Use in a Screens lens |
|---|---|---|
| UiPath OCR template clustering (IoU > 0.9, 70% presence, placeholders, rare-word links) | No; built for Citrix/RDP screenshots | **Direct**: core screen-identity method |
| TalkMiner bounding-box and stability rules | No; lossy webcasts | **Direct**: suppress call tiles, toasts, cursor |
| GIFdroid SSIM change detection; SSIM+ORB identity | Keyframing no; mapping needs a pre-built graph | **Partial**: change detection and visual tiebreaker; clustering replaces mapping |
| Feiz et al. / ViBR / VLM adjudication | No (pixels) | **Adapt**: a frontier VLM names clusters and judges borderline merges |
| Rico template rule; DroidBot content-free hash | Yes (resource IDs, view tree) | **Concept only**: template-level default, rebuilt on stable OCR labels |
| Yandrapally near-duplicate taxonomy | Taxonomy no; crawler yes | **Direct as design vocabulary**: clone / cosmetic / dynamic-data / duplication |
| Collapse consecutive repeats, keep revisits | Analytics are instrumented; rule is not | **Direct** |
| Expected path, direct/indirect success, lostness, pietree | Measured on instrumented tests; maths needs only a sequence | **Direct**, given a researcher-declared expected path |
| Prefix trees (CoreFlow, LifeFlow), swimlanes, MatrixWave | No | **Direct**, counting participants |
| Top-N + Other, frequency sliders, pruned DFGs | n/a | **Avoid** at n = 5–12 |
| Rage, dead and misclicks; per-screen misclick scores | Yes (DOM event stream) | **Unavailable**; at best dwell time and back-and-forth revisits |
| URL/page-title identity | Yes, unless OCR'd from the address bar | **Opportunistic** override when chrome is visible |

## Conclusion

The research changes what kind of problem this is. It looked like a computer-vision problem: build a better "same screen?" detector. The evidence says that detector will plateau around F1 0.65–0.85 whatever signal it uses, and that its threshold will not survive the next study's product. The decisive design moves lie elsewhere. Treat screens as a researcher-editable clustering within each study, defaulting to template level. Treat order as an individual-against-expected comparison, not a mined consensus. Use quotes as the disambiguator that path metrics lack. Instrumented tools cannot copy that last move, because their per-screen data is behavioural, while Bristlenose's is what participants said.

One methodological gap matters most and is cheap to close. No paper evaluates any of these techniques on Teams or Zoom screen-share recordings, at a 4 s cadence, with call-app overlays. Before choosing between the text-template and visual layers, Bristlenose should hand-label screen boundaries and identities in a handful of real sessions and score the UiPath-style template against SSIM and a VLM pass. Rico did this with 1,044 labelled pairs. A few hundred labelled pairs from Bristlenose's own recordings would answer what no source in this survey can.
